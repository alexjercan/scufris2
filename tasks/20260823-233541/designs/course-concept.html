A* Pathfinding for Grid Games

How your units find the shortest route around walls - and how to know when they can't.

Hook

A unit needs to get from one corner of the map to the other, and there is a wall in the way. It could just walk toward the goal and see what happens - that works until it slams into the wall and has to figure out the rest by trial and error. A* instead scores every step by two numbers at once: how far it has already walked, and a quick estimate of how far it has left. That one idea is the difference between a unit that fumbles around obstacles and one that goes straight to the best route [1].

Ladder

How A* gets there

Three ideas, stacked. Each one fixes what broke in the last.

1Chasing the goal is not enough

The simplest search, greedy best-first, always expands whichever open cell looks closest to the goal by straight-line distance, and never looks at how far it has already walked. In open ground that is fine. Below, the only gap in the wall is at the top, but the goal is at the bottom-right - so "closer to goal" pulls greedy straight down first. It has to walk the wrong way, hit the wall, and crawl all the way back up before it even finds the door [2]. It is just as fast as A* here - it explores almost as few cells - it just commits to the wrong route and never reconsiders.

2Remembering the walk so far: g(n)

Dijkstra's algorithm fixes the dead-end problem by tracking g(n), the exact cost of the path from the start to a cell, and always expanding the cheapest one found so far. That guarantees the shortest path [1], but with nothing pointing it toward the goal it spreads out evenly in every direction, like ripples from a stone, even into cells that plainly lead away from the target.

3f(n) = g(n) + h(n): the best of both

A* scores every candidate cell with f(n) = g(n) + h(n): cost already paid, plus an estimate of the cost still owed, and always expands the lowest total next. As long as h(n) never overestimates the true remaining distance - Manhattan distance is a safe choice on a grid where a unit only moves up, down, left, or right [1][3] - the first time A* reaches the goal, that path is provably the shortest one [1].

Cell P looks closer by heuristic alone (lower h) - greedy would pick it. A* adds up the full cost and picks Q instead.

Check

Quick check

Suppose you accidentally implement A* with g(n) hard-coded to 0, so only h(n) ever affects which cell gets expanded next. What happens?

Zeroing out g(n) leaves f(n) equal to h(n), which is exactly greedy best-first from step 1 of the ladder - it still moves toward the goal, but it can no longer tell a cheap route from an expensive one, so it walks into the same dead ends [1][2].
